Around kilometer 20, somewhere deep in the Texas pines, I was having a full-blown crisis of confidence.

My heart rate was perfect. My pacing was exactly where it needed to be. Nutrition on schedule. Physically, I felt controlled and strong. But between my ears? I couldn't shake a deep fear that I simply didn't have what it would take to see the finish line. And I was frustrated, maybe even a little in despair, that the mind game had started this early.

Before that Saturday I had never run more than 50K. I was proud of my sub-6 hour PR from late 2024. Then I came back to this same course a year later for the Rocky 50K and finished 15 minutes slower, spending the last 5K hobbling to the finish with my legs seizing from heat-induced cramps. That was 50 kilometers. And here I was trying to double it on the same course, in eerily similar weather conditions. What in the world was I thinking?

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

---

## The Setup

The day started about as perfectly as you could ask for. Bright, beautiful, springtime weather in classic temperamental Texas fashion, a welcome change from the sub-freezing temperatures just weeks prior. My family and I had arrived the night before to register and set up our crew station at Tyler's Aid Station, as close to the start/finish line as we could manage. The Rocky Raccoon 100 weekend hosts both the 100-mile and 100K events, and I was blown away by how big the 100-mile field was. Crew stations filled up every inch of open area around Tyler's. We secured a spot relatively close, just outside the race lane where I could duck in and out easily.

I had built four years of trail running experience to get to this starting line, with some adventure racing mixed in along the way. This was my new personal frontier.

The course is a 20-mile loop around Lake Raven. Relatively flat by ultra standards, but the terrain gets you: sandy sections, rock-covered jeep track, mountain bike trails, and those infamous root systems throughout the tall Texas pines. The joke is the roots get taller with each lap and your lifting muscles are put to the test as the route twists and turns through the forest. For the 100K, there's a short 3.5km prologue loop to chip off the distance, then three complete 20-mile laps.

![Ready to go!](/blog/images/IMG_6067.jpeg)

I had prepared three nutrition kits for the crew tent and a drop bag at Nature Center Aid Station, which sits near the midway point of each lap. The drop bag had backup nutrition, a first aid kit (bandaids, blister pads, tape, sunscreen) and fresh socks. The plan was to do a sock change at the start of Lap 2 and Lap 3 to stay ahead of blisters, since I'd be in one pair of shoes for the entire race. Each crew kit had written instructions for my family so they'd know what food to load into my pack and what I might want to grab and eat on the move. My goals for the day, in order: arrive at the starting line uninjured; finish the event; aim for 14 hours but stay adaptable.

I also built a simple tracking app for my crew during the taper, a fitting use of my restless energy in those antsy days leading up to the race. I wanted them to be able to easily see when I might arrive at each aid station, rather than relying on the race results website to behave itself. The app looked up my bib number on the results page, captured my last check-in time, and updated estimated arrival times accordingly. I made a companion page for myself that I could pull up in the field to submit my current distance and generate updated estimates on the fly. It proved extremely useful to them, freeing everyone up to enjoy the park and just show up where they needed to be.

## Lap 1

At 6:50am I lined up in the starting chute, deliberately toward the back. I never like being up front. Too much adrenaline, too much pressure to keep up, too easy to get swept up in the crowd. My race logic: being caught behind a slow runner at these distances is a far better problem than overexerting too soon. I kept expecting more people to fill in behind me. I think I was used to the Rocky 50K which draws a larger field. Right at 7:00am, we were off.

The start runs through a hilly section of the park, splitting between the popup canopies and tents of all the crew stations. The atmosphere is electric. Family and friends with homemade signs cheering on their runner and everyone else. It really is something special. The energy always feels like a speed boost in Mario Kart.

I had practiced my pacing during taper and had a good feel for where I needed to be. I had also finally, after many races of frustration, dialed in my Garmin smartwatch with the right data displays: rolling pace, overall time, and heart rate, all on the same screen. My protocols were simple: never let heart rate exceed a certain threshold no matter what; walk all inclines for the first lap; integrate one minute of walking within each 15-minute segment; keep a rolling pace that would support my time goal. My Lap 1 target was the most aggressive but still far slower than my normal zone 2 runs. The challenge was holding back when I felt great and wanted to surge.

The first nutrition kit went to plan. Five Honey Stinger gels, three stroopwafels, eating something every 30 minutes, alternating between gels and solid food, sticking to what I had trained with.

Then, around halfway through Lap 1, the fear set in.

I tried taking in extra nutrition, thinking a hit of carbs might improve my mood. It didn't help. I got a small boost when I emerged from the woods near Nature Center Aid Station and heard my wife's voice: "Is that him?!" My son: "Yep, it is!" My wife and two boys were there waiting. I didn't need anything from my drop bag except the luxurious public park restroom, so I took care of business, grabbed some sliced pickles for sodium, waved them on and headed back out.

The relief was frustratingly short-lived.

I couldn't stop dwelling on the enormity of what I was trying to do and how uncharted the territory was. I focused on prayer. On the fact that I was here, moving, uninjured, and that I had every indication I was prepared for this and virtually no indication otherwise, other than the noise between my ears. So I soldiered on.

When I came through Tyler's at the end of the lap to my crew tent I was resolved but melancholy. My family reset my kit, helped with a sock change, and cheered me on. I grabbed some new food items and took my first planned 100mg caffeine pill of the day. Started to head out.

And then my wife cheered. Loudly. "You've got this, let's go runner!"

Like one bird in a forest calling out to its neighbors, cheers grew out from the tents around us like a wave.

I will never forget that moment. I never played sports as a kid, or at any level, where crowd support was a factor. This was the first time I had ever experienced anything close to that. In an instant, jogging through the last of the makeshift campsites, I felt completely locked in. I had come through a dark patch. I had expected it to hit around kilometer 80. It arrived at kilometer 20. But I was through it.

I would finish this thing.

## Lap 2

I was in a groove.

I had planned for my pace to slow with accumulating fatigue and longer aid station stops, but I was keeping along with my Lap 1 goal and still within heart rate range. I had a mood shift. Got outside my own head. Saw the beauty around me. Started cheering on my fellow runners. I found flow.

Time drifted by and I decided to use headphones for the first time ever in a race. I put on, at very low volume, a 170bpm training album I had made and just let it soak into the background and give my feet a target.

Coming into Nature Aid Station I felt really good overall except for a hot spot developing on my right toe. And with incredible timing, my daughter arrived having just driven in. My wife, two boys, and daughter were all there together to help me with a quick foot dressing. We hugged. Celebrated me crossing 50 kilometers. I giggled to myself, remembering what Sam said to Frodo: "Mr. Frodo, this is the furthest from home we've ever been."

The back half of Lap 2 was a blur, and then once back at the crew station I was feeling determined but honest about the toll the day was taking. The body was starting to speak. Weather heating up. My family was outstanding, handling a complete foot cleaning, checkup, and sock change while I just sat in a chair and took in some variety in food. That's when my stomach started sending signals I hadn't experienced before. Waves of nausea. Nearly 10 hours on a near-100% carb diet was doing something, though I wasn't sure if it was gut issues or the heat starting to catch up with hydration. I was taking in ample sodium and liquid. On paper, doing the right things.

The resupply at the crew tent took longer than I would have liked. Nobody's fault, really, but my mental capacity was diminished and trying to decide how to address how I felt had me moving slowly. Looking back at the data, my first crew stop was just over 10 minutes. This one was about 15. Not a dramatic difference, but in the moment it felt like slow motion and those extra few minutes in the chair left me feeling stiff heading out for the final lap.

---

## Lap 3

My stomach was really not happy.

Waves of nausea kept rolling through. Any time I thought about taking a gel or reaching for Tailwind I nearly gagged. I felt like if I could just let my body do what it needed to do, I'd be better for it. So I pulled off trail, trying to be considerate of anyone coming up behind me, and tried to be sick. I dry heaved three or four rounds. Nothing came up.

But a completely new sense of well-being came over me. I suddenly felt completely normal. Whatever reset needed to happen evidently did. A few nearby runners checked in to see if I needed anything. We had a laugh about the absurdity of this thing we put ourselves through.

I was back in gear and moving.

![In gear](/blog/images/IMG_6179.JPG)

That was also the last time I touched Tailwind. I switched to fresh water for the duration and took one salt tab every 30 minutes. The tiny pancakes at the first aid station stop were amazing. Temperatures started to drop and the muscle cramping signs subsided.

Keeping an eye on my rolling pace, I felt happy about the progress and had no interest in doing anything heroic or stupid. The goal had always been to finish. I knew this was the place things could spiral out of control if I didn't manage the controllables: nutrition, hydration, respect any cramping signals, and stay focused on the micro-goals of each kilometer and each aid station ahead.

I get a huge mental boost on looped courses from saying so long to each element as I pass it for the last time. I crossed one of the park roads and told the young volunteers managing traffic: "Well, guys, this is the last we'll see each other. Thanks for your help." When I came up to the turnaround sign on that long, annoying out-and-back section I flipped it off, because I hated that part of the course. But I never had to see it again. I thanked the workers at Gate Aid Station and bid them farewell. All the "lasts" gave me just enough of a sense that I was nearly there, ticking them off like minor victories.

Around 70km, I was in grind mode. Not totally happy about my life choices up to that point, but still determined.

Then my phone rang.

My running partner and one of my best friends in the world called to cheer me on and tell me how proud he was. JP introduced me to trail running when I went to support him at a race back in 2022. Up until that point, my running experience amounted to one half marathon, an event that had caused me so much pain and suffering by the end that I had sworn I was not built for anything over 13.1 miles. I had sworn off running beyond a friendly neighborhood 5K for several years after that, until I discovered the freedom and beauty of running trail and the outpouring of encouragement among that community. Everything changed.

As JP and I talked, my watch chimed. I had passed 75km. We celebrated another major milestone, another box checked on the way to 100. His call meant the world to me. I experienced another resurgence, fell back into flow, started ticking off more milestones, passing a few more people, and feeling the strongest I had all day.

## The Last 10 Miles

Short of crossing the finish line, the moment I had been looking forward to most all day was having my son Zach meet me at Nature Aid Station to pace the final 10 miles.

Zach is a reasonably fit, nearly 17-year-old with experience on long hikes with his dad. He stays active on his high school tennis team but had never run more than 4 miles. He ran those 4 miles with me the week before in a quick-and-dirty fitness assessment. I figured if he could keep up with me at a faster pace for half the distance, he could handle a 10-mile power hike with some strides at the back end of a hundred kilometer race.

I emerged from the woods just before Nature Aid Station and my family was there in the dark, calling out our last name, "Benson?!", as each headlamp came through the trees. When they spotted me, I could hear the shock in their voices. After seeing me in a focused and serious state most of the day, here I was running out of the woods with a strong stride and a completely upbeat attitude. My wife was blown away and her enthusiasm was infectious. She kept saying she couldn't believe how good I looked. My youngest son looked at me and said: "Dad, you look better than at the start."

What an epic way to check in at the tail end of a race.

Having Zach there to talk with and make me laugh made the time fly by. My running capacity was hitting its limits and we were mostly power hiking by that point, but still moving well enough to pass people along the way. I loved getting to share the experience of that final aid station in the dark with him, colored lights decorating the tent and rock music blasting from speakers. He discovered the warm cups of ramen and we slurped them down together, giddy about how perfect a treat it was at that particular moment in time.

I knew the 14-hour goal wasn't going to happen. But I could beat 15 hours if we kept a smart pace. I committed us to holding a certain rolling pace to beat the clock.

Within a few kilometers of the finish, my legs felt good enough and the cramping signs had subsided enough that I could take on some surges of running. I really wanted to actually run those last couple of kilometers through the campground and up to Tyler's where the finish line party was, not shuffle in, not hobble. Run it in.

Zach and I ran it in, side by side, to the sound of cowbells and cheers and the smiling faces of my wife, daughter, and youngest son waiting at the finish.

I came through at 14 hours and 44 minutes and 40 seconds. 27th overall.

The furthest from "home" I'd ever been.

## What I Learned

The mind game arrived at kilometer 20, not kilometer 80 where I had expected it. That surprised me, and then it didn't. Every race I've done has taught me that the body tends to be more ready than the mind gives it credit for, especially when you've put in the work. The work was done. The preparation was real. The doubt was just noise.

The crew setup made an enormous difference. Having my family there, with pre-packaged kits and written plans so they weren't guessing and I wasn't managing them while trying to manage myself, that investment of time and energy before the race paid off during it. Same with the sock changes. Same with the caffeine timing. Having a plan that I could execute without having to think under fatigue freed up what little mental bandwidth I had left for the things that actually needed it.

The nausea on Lap 3 was a learning. Dry heaving on the side of a trail is a uniquely humbling experience, and also apparently exactly what my body needed. Switching to fresh water and salt tabs for the final stretch worked. I'll know that for next time.

And there will be a next time.

If finishing 50K at sub-6 was proof that I could do this kind of thing, and the Rocky 50K in the heat showed me where the limits were, then this was the day I found out what the edge of the map actually looks like and learned that there is more beyond it. I'm not done exploring.

To my wife, my daughter, Zach, my youngest son, and to JP: thank you. This one belonged to all of us.
